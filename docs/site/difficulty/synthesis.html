What is the difficulty of a task?

A static copy; data as of 22 September 2026.

Difficulty is not a property of a task on its own. It is a property of an encounter: a task met by a population of solvers under a declared frame, which says who attempted it, how many times, and who judged the answers. Change the population or the judge and the number changes, so the frame is part of the definition, never a footnote.

For this project a task's difficulty is the average model's failure rate on it. Take each model's observed failure rate on the task and average them with equal weight, however many attempts each model made. The result is placed on a scale that stretches both ends, so tasks that almost every model solves and tasks that almost every model fails stay readable instead of piling up at the edges.

Three things it is not. It is not one model's failure rate: that number mixes the task with the choice of model. It is not a label such as tricky or underspecified: labels are kept beside the number and decide membership, not position. And it is not how much a task tells us: a task can be exactly as hard as another and be far less informative, because models disagree on it or answer it by chance; that inconsistency is a second number, kept apart.

The estimate follows the definition. A plain average has two defects at the ends, which is where reliability is read: a task no model ever failed reads as zero and cannot be placed on the stretched scale, and rarely attempted tasks land at the ends by chance. So one pseudo-observation, half a failure and half a success, is added against the task's total attempts. The pull it exerts weakens with every attempt any model makes, the estimand stays the equal-weight average, and a never-failed task reads as no failures in that many attempts rather than as impossible to fail. Each position carries an uncertainty, and the Bayesian fit that reads a model's failure curve against the positions is told that uncertainty rather than a sharpened number.

Across the fields that have measured difficulty before us, nearly every measure is one of five things done to the same object: where the task sits on an outcome scale, how consistently solvers succeed on it, what it costs to solve, how much a solver's success on it reveals, and how it compares with other tasks head to head. The catalogue of thirty-nine such measures, each with a critique, is on the measures page; the reasons the alternatives were set aside are in the full synthesis.

If only a handful of numbers per task are kept, keep these: the position on the scale with its uncertainty, the frame it was measured in, the labels that decide membership, and the inconsistency mass. Everything else on these pages is derived from them.
