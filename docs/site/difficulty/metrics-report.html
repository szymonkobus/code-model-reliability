Capability, reliability, and the third axis

A static copy; data as of 22 September 2026.

Fix a population of models: in practice, equal weight over the models with complete runs on both halves of wave 1. Each task then has a difficulty, the average model's failure rate on it, placed on a scale stretched at both ends. Each model has a failure curve over that scale: at each difficulty, the share of its attempts that fail on the tasks there.

Tasks come from a space far larger than any benchmark, so the pages also carry a distribution over that space. Every quantity below is stated for a random task from that distribution and estimated on the tasks used, with their positions carrying the weight. A task whose rank does not change can still move a number when its position does.

Capability is the probability that the model solves a random task. Read through the difficulty scale, it is the area under the model's success curve weighted by how many tasks sit at each difficulty, and the difficulty at which the curve crosses half is the model's capability crossing, the single number the pages report.

Reliability answers a different question: up to what difficulty can the model be trusted. The pages fix a tolerance of one failure in a hundred and report the difficulty at which the average-rate curve crosses it. Two readings of "reliable below that point" are kept, because they answer different questions: the whole workload up to the boundary averaging within tolerance, and every stretch of it staying within tolerance. The reliable zone is what lies below the boundary; the solvable zone is what lies below the point where the model still succeeds at all.

The third axis measures what the first two hide. A model near its boundary does not fail cleanly: it solves a task on some attempts and not on others. The contested mass is the share of the task spectrum in that state. Compressing each task to one number loses something measurable, and the loss splits exactly in two: inconsistency within a difficulty level and spread between levels. The pages report both parts.

The estimation layer is written down in one place: raw frequencies for boundaries and for mass, smoothing only inside difficulty levels, weights from positions rather than ranks, and the Bayesian fit as the estimator of the curves. What this fit does under concentrated failures is measured on synthetic arms and disclosed beside the affected crossings. The candidates that were killed, and the attack that killed each, are in the full report.
