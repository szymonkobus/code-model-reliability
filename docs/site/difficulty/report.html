Defining task difficulty from rollouts

A static copy; data as of 22 September 2026.

A difficulty definition is a rule that turns a task's rollout record into a value or an ordering, with a declared frame: which models, what attempt budget, which judge. Six genuinely different candidates were compared on wave 1, from the plain failure rate through smoothed and frame-declared rates to a joint fit of tasks and models, and each was stressed the same way: add or remove tasks, add or remove models, replicate across independent draws, and inject known defects.

Two layers were adopted from that test. The ranking layer, the axis every page positions tasks on, is the smoothed, frame-declared failure rate: the average model's failure rate on the task with one pseudo-observation against the task's total attempts, flagged where answers were censored. It was the most stable object under task removal, it is computable in one pass over the store, and it needs no fit to exist. The curves layer is the Bayesian fit of each model's failure curve against those positions. It is the privileged estimator for the curves and for the two crossings read off them, and it is a standard diagnostic every task owner runs, but it is not the definition of difficulty.

Three words are reserved. A task's difficulty is the average model's failure rate on it. A model's capability is the difficulty at which it fails half of its attempts on the tasks at that difficulty. Its reliability is the difficulty at which it fails one attempt in a hundred. Both crossings are read on the model's average-rate curve, the failure rate seen across all the tasks at a difficulty; the median-task curve is a second, labelled option that sits lower at the reliability end.

Every served number carries its flags rather than hiding them. Answers cut at the output cap stay in the attempt count with a flag, because a cut answer is not a claim of failure. Plus-test fails that are the judge's time wall on solutions the whole population runs near the limit are the same class and are flagged the same way. A model whose failures concentrate on a few tasks carries a concentration flag on its crossings. A fit whose basis was too small carries a basis flag until its re-fit is measured. A reliability crossing whose spread is wider than one difficulty step reads as not resolved, with its value and spread kept beside it.
